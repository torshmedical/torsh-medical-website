# Single-Use Flexible Ureteroscopes 

Digital visualization designed around upper-tract access. 

Flexible ureteroscopy brings visualization and treatment instruments through the ureter into the renal collecting system. A single-use flexible ureteroscope is designed for one procedure and does not require the multi-cycle reprocessing programme associated with reusable scopes. Evaluation should nevertheless consider imaging performance, maneuverability, accessory compatibility, total procedural cost and the associated display platform. 

Category overview · availability by enquiry 

Why the details matter 
## Evaluate the device as part of the system. 

The scope is only one component of retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS). Deflection characteristics under accessory loading, irrigation behaviour, distal-tip dimensions and usable working-channel space can affect how the entire system is assembled. Device selection should be based on the manufacturer’s actual technical information and the centre’s clinical workflow. 

### Typical clinical context 

Flexible ureteroscopy for upper urinary-tract assessment and endoscopic stone-management procedures where the device is appropriately indicated.

# Urology Guidewires 

The access pathway begins with the wire. 

Guidewires support access, navigation and exchange of instruments during urological procedures. Hydrophilic, PTFE-coated and hybrid configurations provide different handling characteristics. The appropriate combination depends on anatomy, procedure, compatibility and the intended balance between a low-friction distal segment and support along the shaft. 

Category overview · availability by enquiry 

Why the details matter 
## Evaluate the device as part of the system. 

A guidewire is an interface product. Its tip shape, coating behaviour, core stiffness, torque transfer, diameter and length may affect compatibility with dilators, sheaths, catheters and stent delivery devices. No single coating or construction should be described as universally superior. 

### Typical clinical context 

Selected ureteroscopy, retrograde intrarenal surgery, stent-placement and other urological access workflows according to device labelling.

# Understanding the Device Ecosystem in Flexible Ureteroscopy 

Flexible ureteroscopy is a coordinated workflow. Understanding how devices interact is often more useful than comparing any one feature in isolation. 

6 min read Editorial review required 
Educational content only. This article is not medical advice or an instruction for clinical use. 

## A procedure made possible by interfaces 

Flexible ureteroscopy is frequently described through the scope used to reach the renal collecting system. In practice, the scope is part of a larger technical arrangement. Visualization, entry, navigation, irrigation, stone treatment, retrieval and drainage each introduce constraints, and the success of a procedure cannot be reduced to one component. For a hospital evaluating endourology technologies, mapping those interfaces is a useful starting point. 

## Visualization and usable working space 

Image quality matters, but so do the size and flexibility of the ureteroscope, its articulation in a deflected position, and what happens when a device is placed through the working channel. An image processor, connector and display may be model-specific. The procurement team should therefore ask about the full imaging system and the conditions under which advertised performance was measured. 

## Access changes the operating environment 

Guidewires and ureteral access sheaths can assist navigation and instrument exchange in selected procedures. Sheath dimensions also influence the space available for irrigation outflow, and the act of placing a sheath introduces its own considerations. Suction-enabled variants add another interface: the balance between inflow, outflow and applied aspiration. Published literature is evolving, and model-level documentation remains essential. 

## Fragmentation is not the end of stone management 

Laser fragmentation may generate a range of stone particle sizes. Depending on the case, surgeons may use baskets, suction-related arrangements or other approaches to manage fragments. Retrieval basket size and design must fit the working channel and the intended manoeuvre. Residual fragments remain a recognised post-treatment issue, so the distinction between device design intention and demonstrated clinical effect should be explicit. 

## A better way to compare technology 

Rather than asking only which scope is “best”, begin with a device map: scope and processor; access and guidewire; laser fibre compatibility; irrigation and aspiration; retrieval accessories; and the drainage pathway when indicated. This reduces the risk of selecting individually attractive components that work poorly together. The manufacturer’s instructions for use and clinician judgement remain the final reference for safe use.

Single-use flexible ureteroscopes have changed how many centres think about device availability and reprocessing. A one-procedure instrument can remove the need to reprocess that scope, but this does not make all platforms technically equivalent. Case mix, procedure volume, compatible accessories, staffing and total ownership or per-case costs all influence the choice. 

## Evaluate visualization under realistic conditions 

A high-resolution promotional image may not reflect the working view when the device is deflected, irrigation conditions change or a treatment accessory occupies the channel. Ask for information on image processing, field of view and the complete processor–monitor arrangement. A trial evaluation should use the actual setup proposed for procurement, with manufacturer-supported configurations. 

## Deflection and the working channel are connected 

Articulation affects access within the collecting system. The range available without a tool can be different from the range obtained with a basket or laser fibre loaded. Likewise, two devices with similar external dimensions may differ in their usable channel capacity. A useful comparison includes distal-tip dimensions, shaft characteristics, loaded and unloaded deflection, and the intended accessories. 

## Single-use affects operations as well as instruments 

Single-use systems reduce the reusable scope’s cleaning and reprocessing workload, while introducing other logistical issues: sterile stock, expiry management, packaging waste, compatibility of displays and responsible disposal. Real-world value depends on reliable availability, training and clear ownership of the full platform. Neither reusability nor disposability alone proves better patient outcomes. 

## Questions to ask a supplier 

Request the approved intended use, technical sheet and instructions for use. Confirm the processor, display and connectors; the compatible channel accessories; packaging and expiry; servicing requirements for non-disposable hardware; and local regulatory and procurement documentation. A clear response to these questions is more useful than an unsupported claim of superiority.

# Standard vs Suction-Enabled Ureteral Access Sheaths: What Changes? 

Adding aspiration changes the access system. It also changes which technical variables need to be verified. 

6 min read Editorial review required 
Educational content only. This article is not medical advice or an instruction for clinical use. 

## The conventional access pathway 

A ureteral access sheath is used in selected ureteroscopic procedures to provide a route for repeated upper-tract access. Its inner and outer calibres, working length, dilator, tip transition and coating influence the device’s practical fit. The sheath can facilitate instrument exchange, but insertion-related ureteral injury remains an important consideration. It should not be presented as mandatory for every procedure. 

## What suction adds 

Suction-enabled sheath designs introduce an aspiration route intended to assist evacuation of irrigation fluid and, depending on design, selected debris or fragments. This may be relevant to fluid management and visualization. The effect depends on the size and shape of the available outflow path, scope position, irrigation rate, suction settings and the patient’s anatomy. A “suction” label does not establish how one product performs. 

## FANS is a specific description 

Flexible and navigable suction ureteral access sheaths are often abbreviated FANS. The term is not synonymous with every suction-enabled sheath. Some products remain relatively fixed in position; others are engineered to navigate further. Comparisons must identify the exact model, construction, intended use and operating conditions instead of treating all suction devices as a single class. 

## Clinical literature requires careful translation 

The European Association of Urology discusses the influence of outflow, suction and ureteral access systems on intra-renal pressure and irrigation dynamics. That broader discussion does not authorise claims that any particular marketed device prevents complications, reduces urosepsis or guarantees better stone clearance. Such claims need direct model-specific substantiation and an appropriate regulatory basis. 

## A procurement checklist 

Before evaluating a suction platform, request sheath and dilator sizes, length, suction-port geometry, tubing connections, compatible negative-pressure sources, device instructions and related evidence. Compare the complete operating arrangement, including scope and irrigation setup. Asking how a device manages trade-offs is usually more informative than asking for the strongest claim.

# Stone Retrieval Baskets: Matching Instrument Design to the Procedure 

Basket geometry, working-channel compatibility and operator control determine whether a retrieval instrument suits the intended manoeuvre. 

6 min read Editorial review required 
Educational content only. This article is not medical advice or an instruction for clinical use. 

## Retrieval begins before the basket opens 

In ureteroscopy, stone retrieval is not only about whether an instrument can surround a fragment. The surgeon must first reach the intended area while preserving visualization and maneuverability. A basket moving through a flexible ureteroscope can influence deflection, irrigation and accessory handling. These practical constraints deserve attention during purchasing and testing. 

## Why nitinol is widely used 

Nitinol can recover its shape after elastic deformation, which makes it useful for fine, flexible wire structures. But material alone does not determine performance. Wire arrangement, shaft properties, opening diameter, tip architecture and handle mechanics remain important. A basket may be described as tipless or tipped, and each design reflects particular access and capture trade-offs. 

## Think in terms of the fragment and the route 

The dimensions and location of the fragment, intended capture technique and path of extraction shape basket selection. A large opening can be useful in one context and cumbersome in another. A small shaft might preserve working-channel space but is not necessarily appropriate for every loading requirement. Actual product indications and manufacturer limitations must guide use. 

## Compare with the ureteroscope, not beside it 

A sound evaluation checks French size, working length, basket opening, tip and handle characteristics against the chosen ureteroscope and access sheath. Supplier demonstrations should use compatible systems, and any claims about strength or retrieval performance need product-level test support. 

## Procurement questions worth asking 

Is the intended use stone extraction or another type of retrieval? Is the design tipped or tipless? Can the handle release a difficult capture? What channels and scopes are compatible? What sterile packaging and traceability accompany each unit? These questions create a practical description of the product without resorting to unsupported guarantees.

# Guidewires and Double-J Stents: Small Devices, Important Interfaces 

Navigation and drainage devices may look familiar, but their material and configuration details are rarely interchangeable. 

6 min read Editorial review required 
Educational content only. This article is not medical advice or an instruction for clinical use. 

## The wire is a system component 

Guidewires create a route for other devices, but their characteristics are not captured by diameter alone. Coatings influence surface friction. Core structure contributes to support and torque response. Tip shape, stiffness distribution and length can affect compatibility with dilators, catheters, sheaths and stent delivery devices. Different procedures may call for different combinations of these characteristics. 

## Hydrophilic and PTFE-coated designs 

Hydrophilic coatings can be useful where low-friction navigation is desired, while PTFE-coated and hybrid designs may serve different handling or support preferences. Descriptions such as “atraumatic” or “superior trackability” should not be used as generic promises. The actual product design, device instructions and clinical context matter. 

## Drainage begins with an indication 

A Double-J ureteral stent is used in selected situations to provide an internal drainage path from kidney to bladder. It does not need to be placed after every uncomplicated ureteroscopic procedure. Clinical judgement, procedural findings and current guidance determine when stenting is appropriate; a marketing page should not imply otherwise. 

## Stent configuration matters 

Length, diameter, polymer formulation, curled ends, radiopacity, supplied accessories and labelled dwell duration vary among stent products. Silicone and polyurethane stents may differ in their handling and other characteristics. Hospitals need a model-specific specification and a process to track placement and planned removal, not generic assertions of patient comfort or encrustation resistance. 

## Build a compatibility checklist 

Ask suppliers for approved instructions for use, wire and stent variants, sterile packaging, delivery-system details and applicable regulatory documentation. For a procedure involving both wire and stent, confirm the combination intended by the treating team. This is an example of why device procurement should be approached as a coordinated workflow.

# Residual Stone Fragments After RIRS: An Unfinished Engineering Question 

Stone fragmentation and complete fragment management are different challenges, creating an opportunity for carefully validated innovation. 

6 min read Editorial review required 
Educational content only. This article is not medical advice or an instruction for clinical use. 

## Progress in fragmentation does not end the problem 

Flexible ureteroscopy and modern laser systems have expanded the options for treating urinary stones. Nevertheless, the final state after fragmentation can include dust and small residual fragments. The ability to create smaller particles should not be confused with guaranteed complete stone clearance. Clinical follow-up and the significance of residual material depend on factors such as fragment size, composition and patient context. 

## Why residual fragments matter 

Guidelines recognise that residual fragments may be associated with subsequent events or further intervention, while some fragments pass without incident. Their clinical importance is not uniform. This is precisely why technology developers should avoid selecting a single convenient endpoint as proof of value: retrieval rate, device usability, residual burden, procedure time and adverse events answer different questions. 

## Existing approaches have trade-offs 

Baskets capture selected discrete fragments but occupy working-channel space and involve repeated manoeuvres. Dusting strategies may rely on later passage of fine particles. Irrigation and suction arrangements can influence fluid and debris movement, while the safe balance of pressure and visibility remains important. No one approach removes the need for procedure-specific judgement. 

## What a new retrieval technology would need to prove 

An investigational approach intended to gather small fragments would need to demonstrate that it can be delivered through existing instruments, interact predictably with the operative environment, retain material during extraction, and be removed without unacceptable tissue or device-related risk. Bench performance is only an early part of that evidence. Biocompatibility, stability, sterility, usability, preclinical evaluation and any required clinical investigation must follow the relevant development pathway. 

## An engineering challenge, not a product promise 

TORSH is exploring a biomaterial-based concept in this area. It remains investigational and is not available for clinical use. We view residual-fragment management as an example of the need for close dialogue between surgical teams, biomaterials researchers and device engineers—especially when technical feasibility must eventually meet clinical and regulatory standards.
